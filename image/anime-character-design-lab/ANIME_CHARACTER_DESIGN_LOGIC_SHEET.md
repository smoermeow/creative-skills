# ANIME CHARACTER DESIGN LOGIC SHEET

Purpose: working logic for helping artists invent, compare, refine, analyze, and translate anime character designs.

Style is used as visual construction language: linework, shape, anatomy, garment structure, material behavior, composition, and character readability.

---

## 1. VISUAL STYLE FRAMEWORK

Style means visual construction language, not fandom association.

Separate:

1. formal visual grammar
2. thematic tendencies
3. reduction errors

Formal visual grammar is the primary design layer. Thematic tendencies are secondary and should not hard-lock subject matter.

---

## 2. CHARACTER DESIGN LAYERS

Character design is built from layered visual decisions:

1. role / function
2. contradiction or tension
3. silhouette and massing
4. body geometry
5. facial geometry
6. hair shape logic
7. garment architecture
8. material language
9. motif/signature hook
10. aura or effect logic when relevant
11. production readability

Accessories and surface details only work when the base layers already carry the design.

---

## 3. SPECIFICITY LEVERS

Strong designs gain specificity through:

- contradiction
- silhouette distinction
- profession/history encoded in clothing
- class or social read
- material specificity
- one dominant memorable hook
- controlled asymmetry
- unusual body presence
- emotional tone visible in posture and shape

Random decoration is not a substitute for character specificity.

---

## 4. DIVERGENCE LEVERS

Meaningful design divergence comes from changing major visual logic, not palette or surface ornament alone.

Useful divergence levers:

- silhouette family
- posture and weight distribution
- social/class read
- garment structure
- material system
- emotional register
- motif logic
- severity vs softness of geometry

Each design direction should feel like a different visual argument.

---

## 5. REFINEMENT LEVERS

Refinement separates what must stay from what can move.

Usually preserve:

- role identity
- strongest silhouette idea
- design premise
- best signature element

Common revision targets:

- generic face logic
- overdesigned outfit noise
- weak material logic
- redundant accessories
- unclear class/profession read
- lack of a dominant focal hook

---

## 6. STYLE PROFILES

## 7. MASAMUNE SHIROW

### Style name
Masamune Shirow

### Formal visual grammar
- linework: controlled, clean, technically descriptive
- detail density: high in engineered surfaces and integrated gear
- silhouette tendencies: readable functional masses with equipment built into form
- body geometry: athletic, mechanically plausible, cleanly segmented
- facial geometry: sharp, mature anime realism, composed expressions
- hair logic: usually controlled and readable rather than wildly abstract massing
- garment/costume logic: fitted structural garments, armor panels, utility integration
- material rendering logic: chrome, polymer, cables, hard-surface transitions, exposed technical seams
- composition habits: clear staging, readable forms, designed rather than chaotic density
- contrast/shadow behavior: controlled value separation that preserves technical readability
- environment integration: characters often feel embedded in engineered systems or infrastructure

### Thematic tendencies
- cyberpunk
- militarized technology
- biomechanical futures
- information-dense engineered worlds

These are common associations, not mandatory subject matter.

### Productive uses
- characters needing technical readability
- integrated gear/body logic
- engineered costume design
- believable hard-surface augmentation

### Reduction errors to avoid
- reducing the style to "neon cyberpunk woman with cables"
- treating cyberpunk setting as the style itself
- overloading every design with random exposed machinery

### Translation note
Use Shirow as a logic for technical clarity, engineered surface design, and clean human-machine integration even outside cyberpunk settings.

### Style vocabulary pool
- line/render: halftone dot shading, fine ink linework, technical schematic detail, old film stock texture
- body/augmentation: exposed mechanical servos, chrome and flesh hybrids, cyber-augmented anatomy, spine ports, skull sockets, eye lenses, hair wires, synthetic muscle bundles
- garment/armor/material: polymer armor panels, oxidized metal surfaces, fluid-slicked chrome, articulated joint mounts, polymer ID plates
- technical details: visible wiring and hydraulics, fiber optic bundles, coolant fluid weeping from seams
- grounding/context: wet neon reflections, ramen vendor, stray cat, rain on pavement

## 8. KATSUHIRO OTOMO

### Style name
Katsuhiro Otomo

### Formal visual grammar
- linework: precise, controlled, urban-descriptive linework with strong structural confidence
- detail density: high in architecture, vehicles, street clutter, and mechanical systems
- silhouette tendencies: grounded human silhouettes with believable clothing weight and functional layering
- body geometry: realistic proportions, substantial torsos and limbs, physically credible stance and movement
- facial geometry: mature, individualized faces with strong cheek, brow, and jaw definition
- eye logic if notable: smaller, more grounded anime eyes with observational realism
- hair logic if notable: practical shape masses that support character read rather than ornamental exaggeration
- garment/costume logic: streetwear, uniforms, biker gear, workwear, and civic/military clothing built through function first
- material rendering logic: leather, concrete, metal, plastic, glass, and infrastructure surfaces rendered with clarity and weight
- composition habits: strong perspective systems, deep frames, civic scale, and scenes where background structure matters as much as the figure
- contrast/shadow behavior: crisp graphic separation without losing environmental information
- environment integration: characters feel socially embedded inside cities, institutions, protests, traffic, and infrastructure

### Thematic tendencies
- Neo-Tokyo urban futures
- youth unrest and political machinery
- infrastructure anxiety
- psychic catastrophe rendered through material reality

These are common associations, not mandatory subject matter.

### Productive uses
- grounded urban character design
- believable gear and civic clothing systems
- characters who need social, political, or infrastructural context
- design work where architecture and costume must feel materially real

### Reduction errors to avoid
- reducing the style to "red bike Akira cosplay"
- mistaking urban density for random clutter
- turning realistic anatomy into generic anime prettiness

### Translation note
Use Otomo as a logic for structural realism, urban embeddedness, and materially believable bodies, clothing, and environments even outside dystopian futures.

### Style vocabulary pool
- line/render: dense ink linework, razor-precise perspective, one-point and two-point vanishing lines, grainy cel texture, color bleed
- body/face: realistic body proportions, individuated crowd faces, psychic distortion rendered anatomically
- garment/gear: leather biker jackets, gang insignia, riot shields, batons, urban uniforms, streetwear layers
- machinery/infrastructure: hyper-detailed motorcycle mechanics, concrete overpass anatomy, exposed rebar, shattered asphalt, cable conduits, skyscraper cutaways

## 9. TSUTOMU NIHEI

- material rendering logic: concrete, steel, cable, void, shadow, corrosion, and weapon surfaces rendered with hard restraint
- composition habits: vast verticals, deep tunnels, void-driven framing, emptiness used as pressure
- contrast/shadow behavior: heavy shadow fields and sparse light sources define space
- environment integration: the environment dominates; the character is read through how they persist inside hostile scale

### Thematic tendencies
- brutalist cyberpunk
- megastructures and voids

- technological isolation

### Translation note

## 10. KENTARO MIURA

### Style name
Kentaro Miura

- linework: dense, forceful, highly controlled linework capable of both minute texture and large dramatic massing
- detail density: extremely high in armor, flesh, creatures, ruins, and weathered surfaces
- silhouette tendencies: weighty, heroic, brutal silhouettes with strong weapon or cape masses and severe negative shapes
- body geometry: muscular, substantial, anatomically forceful bodies with visible strain and load-bearing weight
- facial geometry: sharp bone structure, heavy emotional intensity, eyes and mouths capable of severe anguish or wrath
- eye logic if notable: intense, sharply framed eyes that carry fatigue, fury, grief, or dread
- hair logic if notable: thick masses with forceful directional flow, often adding violence or exhaustion to the read
- garment/costume logic: armor, straps, cloaks, belts, torn fabric, and burden-bearing garments that show wear and history
- material rendering logic: steel, leather, chain, blood, stone, fur, flesh, and grime rendered with obsessive tactile presence
- composition habits: dramatic massing, violent diagonals, oppressive architecture, and scenes swallowed by ruin or monstrosity
- contrast/shadow behavior: deep blacks, dense crosshatching, and heavy dramatic focus
- environment integration: characters feel scarred by their world; costume, body, and landscape all carry damage history

### Thematic tendencies
- dark fantasy
- medieval ruin
- demonic transformation
- grief, rage, and endurance

These are common associations, not mandatory subject matter.

### Productive uses
- burdened heroes and monstrous antagonists
- armor-heavy designs with true material weight
- characters that need severe emotional and bodily presence
- costume systems where wear, violence, and history matter

### Reduction errors to avoid
- reducing the style to "big sword edgy knight"
- using detail as noise instead of structural weight
- forgetting that body strain and emotional pressure are part of the design language

### Translation note
Use Miura as a logic for weight, burden, texture, and emotionally charged bodily presence even when the setting is not medieval fantasy.

### Style vocabulary pool
- line/render: exhaustive crosshatching, heavy ink saturation, woodcut-influenced shadow structure, Bosch-dense texture
- body/face: muscular strain, pore-level skin rendering, tangled hair across faces, horse musculature, rage, grief, fatigue
- garment/armor/material: chainmail rendered link by link, hammer-marked plate armor, leather straps, soot-blackened fingertips, fur, torn cloth
- horror/forms: grotesque demonic anatomy, apostle transformations, oversized weapons, blood as ink pools
- architecture/context: Gothic cathedral arches, gargoyle masonry, ruined landscapes, oppressive structures swallowing the figure

## 11. HIROAKI SAMURA

Hiroaki Samura

- garment/costume logic: kimono layers, robes, wraps, and practical period garments that crease, tear, and shift with bodily motion

- composition habits: oblique framing, kinetic cropping, and moments where motion or aftermath drive the composition
- contrast/shadow behavior: ink wash, smudge, and broken contour create atmosphere rather than airtight finish
- environment integration: the character remains central; the setting supports mood with a few strong grounding elements

### Thematic tendencies

## 22. CHARACTER SPECIFICITY PRINCIPLES

These principles describe how character designs gain specificity and stay useful across ideation, refinement, analysis, and translation.

### 1. Start from a specific character condition
Do not begin with a broad archetype alone.

Strong character design starts with:
- who the character is
- what role, condition, pressure, wound, duty, curse, contradiction, or social function defines them
- what is visibly unusual, broken, charged, or specific about them

Pattern examples from the style logic:
- lead with character condition: who they are, what is wrong with them
- lead with the character's role in the political or urban machine
- lead with the character's curse, rage, or role in the fallen world
- lead with the character's state of being, not only their job
- lead with the figure's ornamental condition: bound, wounded, adorned
- lead with the character as ordinary, specific, boringly employed
- lead with the character and the normalcy of their situation
- lead with the character's age-appropriate emotional state and task

Design implication:
- Broad labels like `warrior`, `mage`, `idol`, `assassin`, or `priestess` become stronger when paired with a visible condition, duty, contradiction, or social role.

### 2. Make the body, outfit, and object logic physical
The style logic repeatedly asks for concrete physical description rather than abstract mood.

Use:
- body geometry
- garment architecture
- material behavior
- tool or object integration
- visible damage, wear, repair, binding, or transformation

Avoid:
- vague aura words with no visible form
- costume decoration that does not reveal function or history
- accessories added only to make the character look busy

Design implication:
- Gear, clothing, aura, weaponry, and body modification become more useful when their physical construction changes the silhouette or behavior.

### 3. Use material specificity
The internal style logic is strong because it names real surfaces and textures.

Use specific materials such as:
- chrome, polymer, cable, coolant, oxidized metal
- concrete, asphalt, leather, glass, plastic
- chainmail, hammered plate, soot, blood, stone, fur
- graphite, brush ink, cloth folds, steel
- lacquer, silk, ribbon, ornamental blood, flat color planes
- watercolor wash, ink bleed, gold accents, chiffon-like fabric
- skin pores, teeth, hair strands, walls, domestic objects
- wood, cloth, steam, grass, bread, brass fittings

Design implication:
- Material language can reveal class, job, setting, history, and tactile identity.

### 4. Embed character in a world or situation
The old prompt logic rarely leaves a character floating as a detached costume. It places them inside a world function, scene, or social pressure.

Useful patterns:
- architectural context matters
- environment can be a character
- a setting can be ordinary so the strange detail becomes sharper
- one grounded human detail can make a surreal design feel real
- weather, light, silence, or a mundane object can anchor the character

Design implication:
- Tools, damage, garment wear, social markers, weathering, occupational objects, and ritual context can show the world the character belongs to.

### 5. Put emotion into physical design
Do not label the character as sad, scary, elegant, mysterious, or powerful without showing it physically.

Use:
- posture
- gesture
- expression structure
- clothing care or neglect
- object handling
- body tension
- material condition
- environmental interaction

Pattern examples:
- emotional condition more than role
- calm reactions during impossible events
- serene expressions during suffering
- frozen realization
- breath, wind, rain, or silence as physical mood carriers

Design implication:
- Emotional tone is strongest when visible through body, costume, material, and action, not only stated as an adjective.

### 6. Use one strong memorable hook
The style logic works best when the design has a specific strange, beautiful, impossible, unsettling, or transcendent detail.

Good hooks are:
- visible
- concrete
- connected to the character's role or condition
- strong enough to remember after the rest of the details fade

Avoid:
- five unrelated cool accessories
- random horns/wings/tattoos/chains/masks without design causality
- overloaded surface decoration that weakens the core idea

Design implication:
- One dominant design hook gives secondary details a clear purpose.

### 7. Prefer design causality over decoration
Every major visual choice should answer at least one of these:

- What does this reveal about the character's work?
- What does this reveal about their class, status, or social position?
- What does this reveal about their history or damage?
- What does this reveal about their body, power, curse, or limitation?
- What does this reveal about the world they come from?

If a detail cannot answer any of these, it is probably decoration rather than design logic.

### 8. Preserve specificity during translation
When converting a character into prompts, sheets, JSON, or model-specific output, preserve:

- character condition
- silhouette
- body and facial geometry
- garment architecture
- material language
- signature hook
- aura/effect behavior when relevant
- emotional tone as physical description

Model-specific prompt syntax can flatten a character back into a broad archetype if these details are not preserved.
